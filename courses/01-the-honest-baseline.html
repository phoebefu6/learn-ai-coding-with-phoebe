The honest baseline

One well-known study measured experienced developers as nineteen percent slower with AI, while those same developers believed they had been twenty percent faster. Another measured fifty-six percent faster. Both are real, both are honest, and the researchers behind the first have since published a partial walk-back of their own headline. Tonight you learn to read that evidence properly, and then you run code in the browser to see where the gap actually lives.

Part 1 · covers the evidence

The most-cited number in this field, and its own authors' correction

8 min live

In July 2025, METR published a randomised controlled trial that landed hard, because the result was the opposite of what everybody expected. It is worth knowing properly, and it is worth knowing what happened next.

LiveWhat the study actually did3 min▶

Sixteen developers with moderate AI experience completed 246 real tasks on mature open-source projects, averaging over 22,000 stars and a million lines of code, which they had contributed to for years. Tooling was primarily Cursor Pro with Claude 3.5 and 3.7 Sonnet.

The three numbers, verbatim from the abstract: they forecast that AI would reduce completion time by 24 percent. Afterwards they estimated it had reduced it by 20 percent. It increased completion time by 19 percent. Expert forecasters in economics and machine learning had predicted 38 to 39 percent speedups.

The confidence interval, plus 2 to plus 39 percent, is not in the paper. It appears on METR's own follow-up page, so cite it there.

Real world

The authors were careful about scope in a way that most people quoting them are not: "We do not claim that our developers or repositories represent a majority or plurality of software development work," and the result may not hold "for less experienced developers, or for developers working in an unfamiliar codebase."

Self-studyThe walk-back, in METR's own words5 min▶

In February 2026 METR published a design update reporting a larger follow-up: 57 developers, 143 repositories, over 800 tasks, ten of whom were in the original study.

  • For the returning original developers: 18 percent slower, confidence interval minus 38 to plus 9 percent.
  • For newly recruited developers: 4 percent slower, confidence interval minus 15 to plus 9 percent.
  • Both intervals cross zero, which means neither result is distinguishable from no effect.

They name the cause as severe selection bias: developers refused to work without AI, and self-selected which tasks to bring. In their words, "the true speedup could be much higher among the developers and tasks which are selected out of the experiment."

And their current position, which is the sentence to remember:

★ METR, February 2026 "Based on conversations with study participants, we believe it is likely that developers are more sped up from AI tools now - in early 2026 - compared to our estimates from early 2025." "Due to the severity of these selection effects, we are working on changes to the design of our study."

This course teaches the 19 percent figure with this attached, because a result quoted without its correction is not evidence, it is a talking point.

Part 2 · covers what practitioners report

Almost right, but not quite

5 min live

Set the studies aside and ask 49,000 developers. The Stack Overflow 2025 survey has the clearest picture of what daily use actually feels like, and the trend is not the one the marketing suggests.

LiveUsage is up and trust is down, at the same time4 min▶
  • 80 percent of developers now use these tools in their workflow.
  • Trust in AI accuracy fell from 40 percent to 29 percent year over year. Positive favorability fell from 72 to 60.
  • The number one frustration, named by 45 percent: "AI solutions that are almost right, but not quite."
  • 66 percent say they spend more time fixing almost-right AI-generated code.
  • 75 percent would still ask another person when they do not trust the answer.

That third bullet is the whole problem in one phrase. Code that is obviously wrong costs you nothing, because you throw it away in four seconds. Code that is almost right is expensive, because you have to read it carefully enough to find the part that is not.

DORA's version of the same finding, at team scale. Their 2025 report on roughly 5,000 professionals found AI now has a positive relationship with throughput, and still has a negative relationship with delivery stability. Their explanation: "an increase in change volume leads to instability" without strong automated testing and fast feedback. And the line worth putting on a wall: "AI doesn't fix a team; it amplifies what's already there."

Build-along

Five real tasks, really executed

★ 22 min · everyone builds

The playground below compiles and runs real JavaScript against a real test suite in this page. Every pass and fail is an actual result. What the levers model is which code a differently-worded ask tends to produce, and the last one is a trap.

Start with everything off and read the code. Zero of five tasks pass. Click through all five and look at what actually broke: median sorts as strings so it returns 9 for [10,9,8], retry swallows the error and returns undefined, slugify leaves the punctuation in.

Switch on "write the spec before the prompt". Five of five. One lever, and it is the difference between naming your edge cases and letting the model choose them for you.

Switch on "give it the surrounding code". The score does not move, and the amber note disappears. That note is the honest part: no test can tell you that the code ignored your conventions and invented a third way of doing something. It passes and your codebase gets worse.

Now switch on the trap: accept everything. Five of five, all green. Then read the code and the red warning underneath it. Every one of those implementations is wrong.

Click through all five and find each flaw. formatBytes returns "undefined" for a big enough number. parseRange silently returns an empty array for a reversed range. retry calls your API one extra time. slugify leaves a trailing dash on any title ending in punctuation. All four pass the shipped suite.

With the trap still on, switch on "write the tests first". The same code drops from five of five to one of five. Nothing about the code changed. The only thing that changed is that the tests were written from the spec instead of after the fact.

That last step is not a metaphor, it is a measured finding. A 2026 study found that tests generated after faulty code detect 14 percent of faults, while tests generated independently detect 25 percent. Their explanation is exactly what you just watched: "incorrect implementations and tests are mutually consistent, masking defects rather than revealing them."

Homework

Before session 2

25 min
  1. Pick the feature. One real thing from your backlog, small enough to finish in six sessions. That is the artifact.
  2. Run your own honest baseline. Next time you use an assistant on a real task, note the time before you start and the time you actually merged, including review and rework. One data point is not a study, but it is yours.
  3. Write the spec before the prompt, once. Name the edge cases: empty input, the boundary value, the error path. Then prompt. Compare it to how you normally work.
  4. Count your almost-rights. Over a week, tally the times you accepted something and then had to go back and fix it. That number is your personal version of the 66 percent.
  5. Optional but genuinely worth it: read METR's February 2026 update. It is short, and watching researchers revise their own famous result in public is a better lesson in evaluating evidence than any blog post about AI.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 1 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · One study measures 55.8 percent faster and another measures 19 percent slower. What is the best explanation?

Both are honest. The 55.8 percent study asked recruits to write an HTTP server from scratch. METR gave experienced maintainers real issues in repositories they had worked in for years. The setting is the variable.

2 · What did METR publish in February 2026 about their own 19 percent slowdown finding?

Not a retraction, a revision. 57 developers, 143 repos, 800+ tasks. The original cohort came out at minus 18 percent with an interval of minus 38 to plus 9, and new recruits at minus 4 percent. They cite severe selection bias and are redesigning the study.

3 · In the playground, accepting everything scored five out of five while every implementation was wrong. Why did the tests not catch it?

The code really executed and really passed. A green suite proves the tests ran, not that the code is right. Switching on tests-written-first took the same code from five of five to one of five.
