The review problem

Reading the diff is the step everyone skips, and it is the only step that catches the things tests do not. Not because your tests are bad, but because a test can only ask the questions someone already thought to ask. Tonight you get a checklist you will reuse for years, you run it on the code you actually wrote for your feature, and you find something. Everybody finds something. That is the point of the exercise, not a sign you did it wrong.

Part 1 · covers the failure mode

Almost right is the expensive kind

5 min live

Obviously wrong code is free. You look at it, you bin it, you have lost four seconds. Almost-right code costs you an unbounded amount of attention, because the only way to find the wrong part is to read all of it carefully enough that you would have noticed.

🎫 Clear all three quiz questions to stamp your passport Live = presented in the 45 minutes Self-study = the full depth, after class

★ What you walk out with tonight. A six-point diff checklist you can apply to any change from any source, one real defect found in your own feature, and a halfway scorecard that tells you honestly where the build stands with three sessions to go.

LiveWhat 49,000 developers say the problem actually is3 min▶

The Stack Overflow Developer Survey 2025 asked more than 49,000 developers what frustrates them about these tools. The top answer, named by 45 percent, was not hallucination and not cost. It was "AI solutions that are almost right, but not quite."

And 66 percent said they spend more time fixing almost-right AI-generated code. Read those two together and you have the economics of this session. The failure mode people complain about is not the one that looks like failure.

It also explains a pattern you have probably felt. The tool is genuinely good, the output is genuinely plausible, and your afternoon still disappeared. Nothing broke loudly. You just spent two hours discovering which of the forty plausible lines was the wrong one.

Part 2 · covers the checklist

What to actually look for in a diff

6 min live

"Review it properly" is useless advice because it does not tell you where to point your eyes. Six places, in this order, every time. It takes about four minutes on a normal change and it is the same six whether a person or a model wrote the code.

LiveThe six points, in the order they pay off5 min▶

1. The edge cases the spec named. Not all edge cases, the ones you wrote down in session 2. Empty input, the boundary value, the maximum. Open the spec next to the diff and tick them off one at a time. If you did not write a spec, this step is where you discover that.

2. The error paths. Follow every call that can fail. What happens when it does? A surprising amount of generated code has a happy path and a shrug. Look for swallowed exceptions, an error logged and then ignored, and a failure that quietly returns a default the caller cannot distinguish from a real answer.

3. The boundary conditions. Off by one, inclusive versus exclusive, the last element, the reversed range, the retry that runs one extra time. These are the classic almost-rights: the logic is correct in shape and wrong by exactly one.

4. Every new import is a package that really exists. Not a package that sounds right. Look it up. This is thirty seconds per import and session 4 will explain why it is the single highest-value thirty seconds in this list.

5. Did it invent a helper you already have? Search the codebase for the function it just wrote before you accept it. This is the failure you will find most often and the one nobody looks for, because a duplicate helper is not a bug in any sense your tests understand.

6. Is the change bigger than the task needed? Extra abstraction, a config option nobody asked for, three files touched when one would do. Scope creep in a diff is not generous, it is surface area you now maintain.

Real world

A data engineer asks for a retry wrapper. It comes back correct, readable, and it also adds a small caching layer "since you will probably want it". Nothing is wrong. Eight months later a stale cache causes an incident, and the person debugging it cannot find anybody who remembers asking for a cache. Point 6 is that story, prevented.

Self-studyHow to read a diff when it is 400 lines4 min▶

The honest answer is that you often cannot, and the right move is upstream: ask for smaller changes. A four-hundred-line diff you skim is worse than four hundred lines you never generated, because now it is merged and it has your name on it.

When you are stuck with a large one, review in passes rather than top to bottom. First pass, imports and dependencies only. Second pass, error handling only. Third pass, the two or three functions that carry the actual logic. Skimming a whole diff once feels thorough and finds nothing. Reading a third of it three times with a single question in mind finds things.

The tell that you are not really reviewing. If you have not paused on a single line, you are reading for shape rather than for meaning. A real review pass produces at least one question, even when the answer turns out to be fine.

Part 3 · covers the duplication pattern

Duplication instead of refactoring

6 min live

Point 5 on the checklist deserves its own part, because there is measurement behind it. GitClear analysed 211 million changed lines authored between 2020 and 2024, and what they found is a shift in the shape of how code changes, not a claim about anyone's quality.

LiveThe numbers, and what they are and are not evidence of5 min▶

Moved lines are the signature of refactoring. When you pull a block into a function and call it from three places, those lines move. GitClear measured moved lines falling from 24.1 percent of changed lines in 2020 to 9.5 percent in 2024, while copy and pasted lines rose from 8.3 to 12.3 percent. In their words, "2024 marked the first year GitClear has ever measured where the number of 'Copy/Pasted' lines exceeded the count of 'Moved' lines."

The sharper number is about individual commits. Commits containing a duplicate block of five lines or more went from 0.45 percent in 2022 to 6.66 percent in 2024. That is roughly one commit in fifteen, up from one in two hundred.

How to hold this honestly. GitClear is independent of the AI vendors, though it does sell code-analytics tooling, and a correlation across an industry is not a causal claim about your team. Teach it as a thing to watch for in your own diffs, not as a prophecy about the profession. The useful version is small and personal: this week, in your commits, did anything get extracted, or did everything get added?

The mechanism was in session 2, and it is not mysterious. An assistant that cannot see the file containing your helper cannot suggest reusing it. It suggests writing one. Multiply that by every task for a year and you get exactly this shape.

Part 4 · covers the vendors' own words

What the vendors say about review

3 min live

You would expect the companies selling these tools to be the last people telling you to slow down and read. Their documentation says otherwise, in plain language, and that is worth more than any warning from a critic.

LiveGitHub and Anthropic, verbatim3 min▶
★ GitHub, responsible use documentation "You should always carefully review and test code generated by Copilot." "Copilot code review should be supplemented with careful human code review." It should "supplement human reviews, not replace them." Copilot "may highlight problems in reviewed code that do not exist."

That last line is the one people skip past, and it matters for how you use an AI reviewer. It produces false positives as well as misses, which means an automated review is a second opinion rather than a verdict. If you resolve its comments without thinking, you have added a step and removed a judgement.

★ Anthropic, Claude Code security documentation "Claude Code only has the permissions you grant it. You're responsible for reviewing proposed code and commands for safety before approval."

Two vendors, two products, same sentence underneath. The responsibility does not transfer. It is worth saying out loud in a team setting, because the quiet assumption in most rooms is that somebody else, or something else, is checking.

Build-along

Review your own diff, then hunt for duplication

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Open the diff you brought from session 2 and the spec you wrote for it. Six points, then a duplication hunt. Everyone finds at least one thing. Finding two is normal and finding none usually means the pass was too fast.

Minutes 1 to 8 · the six points, in order. Spec open on one side, diff on the other. Edge cases, error paths, boundaries, imports, existing helpers, scope. Write each finding down as one line. Do not fix anything yet, because fixing breaks the pass.

Minutes 9 to 14 · the duplication hunt. For every new function in the diff, search the codebase for its name and for one distinctive line from its body. Twice is a coincidence, three times is a helper you should have called.

Minutes 15 to 18 · classify what you found. Split your findings into two piles: things a test would have caught, and things no test would ever catch. The second pile is why this session exists, and it is usually the bigger one.

Minutes 19 to 22 · fix one, and note the rest. Fix the highest-value finding now. Put the others in a list. You are three sessions in with a real feature half built, which is exactly where you should be.

There is a written spec for this feature that you could hand to someone else Half marks for bullets in a scratch file. It counts if it exists outside your head.
You can name the three files that matter, without opening the repo If not, that is a five-minute fix tonight, not a failure.
You have read every line you have merged so far Honestly. Most people score one here at the halfway point, and that is what halfway means.
Nothing in the diff duplicates something the codebase already had You only know this if you searched. Not searching scores zero, not one.
The feature is smaller than it was when you picked it, or the same size Growing scope is the most common halfway problem, and it is easy to reverse now.
Homework

Before session 4

25 min
  1. Run the six points on one change a day for a week. Yours or a colleague's, generated or hand-written. The checklist is not an AI checklist, and using it on human code is how it becomes a habit rather than a ritual.
  2. Keep a findings tally. Two columns: caught by a test, caught only by reading. After a week, the ratio between those columns is the most useful number you will produce in this course.
  3. Do one duplication sweep. Pick the file you have changed most this month and look for two functions that do nearly the same thing. Extracting one of them is a good use of twenty minutes.
  4. Verify every import in your feature. Every one, by looking it up rather than by recognising the name. Bring the list to session 4, because session 4 is about exactly what happens when one of them is not real.
  5. Optional. Read GitHub's responsible use page on agents. It is short, and reading a vendor tell you to supplement its review with careful human review lands differently in their words than in mine.
